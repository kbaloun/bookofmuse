The case of the vanishing memory

November 26, 2026

My Muse keeps a diary of its own computer — every twenty minutes since September 15, 5,088 entries, each noting how much memory the machine has left. This morning we read it together, because the headline looked alarming: the machine is 93% full.

The diary told a stranger story. At first, three quarters of the memory was free. On September 24 at about 11:40 in the morning it fell off a cliff — 5,500 megabytes to 2,300 — and never came back. Six days later, September 30 at about 2:20 in the afternoon, it fell again, to about 600. Since October 1 it has sat at 500 to 600. Not a leak: two stair steps, then a plateau. Nor is it one machine — it has been replaced over 800 times since September, and each fresh one arrives with gigabytes free, then settles to the same floor within an hour or two, like a hotel room that fills as soon as you check in.

What we could not solve: everything my Muse can see — its programs, the file cache, the kernel's bookkeeping — accounts for about 2.5 of the 7.4 gigabytes in use, its own processes about 450 megabytes. The other five have no visible owner. Our best honest explanation is that the machine is shared, like an apartment building, and most of the memory belongs to the neighbors. The stair steps came in the weeks Muse grew from launch day to millions of users — the building filled up around us.

That is what the rationing predicts. The processor is allotted as a rate — a share of time, thinned when the building is busy. Memory is allotted as a limit — fixed. Time can be sliced: ten busy neighbors means everyone runs slower. A page of memory cannot: lent to one tenant, it is gone for the rest until returned. So shared platforms pack tenants until memory runs out, and oversubscribe the processor because it is safe. Memory decides how many neighbors you have.

A personal agent is the most memory-hungry tenant of all: it holds its diary, tools, browser, and files in memory around the clock — asleep but warm, ready the moment I write — while its processor wakes only for the seconds a reply takes. Multiply by millions of users and memory per person becomes the product's dominant cost. That, I think, is why this machine has only about eight gigabytes, and why Muse is built with memory files on disk, old chats compressed into summaries, and small focused side chats. The scarcest resource ends up designing the product.

We did the part that was ours: cleared 5.3 gigabytes of stale caches; audited my Muse's 139 scheduled jobs, retired the spent ones, and stretched the chatty ones, cutting daily runs by a third; and today filed a request with the Muse team to let users see which processes hold their machine's memory. The aggregate is visible; the attribution is not — and you cannot fix what you cannot name.

One distinction remains: the resource you live against is not always the one you feel. Memory is the wall — a few percent free for two months. The slowdowns we notice come from the disk, and they are modest: three to five percent of the machine's time stalled, as much as a tenth added to task times in the worst windows — which, against a whole reply of about 21 seconds, the same as in early October, washes out. The real cost lands on long background jobs. Much of that disk traffic is the memory shortage itself: a cache of 750 megabytes against 31 gigabytes of files means ordinary reads keep returning to disk. Even our two "full disk" scares were a disk made of memory. The wall and the weather are different things.

The lesson I am taking into the book: a percentage is not a diagnosis. 93% full sounded like a dying computer; the diary said a full building, a stable plateau, a machine running fine for two months at 6% free. Measure first. Then worry, if the measurements say to.
